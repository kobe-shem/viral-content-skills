# Organic ideation: furniture repair

Fictional brief and product. Fresh-v2 candidate; independent review marked usable, with production/taste caveats. No owner approval, recorded timing or performance result.

1. Find the moving joint before planning the repair
Opening picture: A chair stands on the workbench. One hand steadies a leg while the other gently moves the adjoining rail; the camera is close enough to see movement at their connection.
Exact opening: “The chair wobbles. Which joint is actually moving?”
Useful body: Demonstrate on an unloaded workshop chair. Stabilize one member, gently move the adjoining member, and watch the seam. Inspect one connection at a time, marking visible movement with removable tape. Show a joint that stays still beside the moving one; explain that movement elsewhere on the chair doesn’t identify its source.
Payoff: Return to the opening joint and repeat the isolated movement with the tape marker visible.
Exact closing: “Mark where the movement happens. That’s the connection to investigate before choosing a repair.”
Text: “Locate the movement.” Keep the decisive close-up uninterrupted; do not imply the inspection alone establishes that the chair is safe.

2. Choose the clamp by where its jaws land
Opening picture: Two differently shaped clamps beside a dry workshop assembly. Place the first clamp against it: one jaw cannot sit squarely because the shape obstructs it. Do not tighten.
Exact opening: “Before you tighten a clamp, look at where both jaws land.”
Useful body: Use the same assembly for both clamps. Show the contact points and the intended closing direction. Replace the first with a clamp whose reach and jaw placement fit this assembly. Add protective scrap pads, align the jaws, then apply light pressure while watching the joint. Explain that clamp choice depends on reaching the contact points and pressing the joint in the intended direction.
Payoff: Hold a close side view of the fitted clamp, both contact points and the joint together.
Exact closing: “Choose the shape that fits the assembly, then check what moves as you tighten.”
Text: “Reach → contact → closing direction.” Label the comparison “This assembly,” rather than declaring one clamp universally better.

3. Make the stain decision on a prepared offcut
Opening picture: Hold a stain container’s color label beside a bare offcut from the project. Put the brush over the scrap before it approaches the furniture.
Exact opening: “Use the label to choose a candidate. Use this scrap to choose the color.”
Useful body: Divide a matching offcut into a bare comparison area and a test area. Prepare the test area as planned for the furniture, then apply the chosen stain according to its instructions. Label the sample with the stain and preparation used. Show the wet application, then cut explicitly to the same sample after its specified drying period; compare it beside the bare area under the same light.
Payoff: Place the dried sample against the project so the viewer sees the actual color decision. If a topcoat is planned, carry the sample through that step before the final comparison.
Exact closing: “Test the finish you plan to use, on matching scrap, before committing the whole piece.”
Text: “Same wood. Same preparation. Planned finish.” Mark the time jump “After drying”; do not suggest the stain dried during the cut.
