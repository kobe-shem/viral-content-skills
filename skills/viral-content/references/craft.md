# Craft decisions

These are conditional working principles, not a formula that guarantees reach. Source IDs and evidence scope live in [sources.md](sources.md).

## Idea and audience

A promising topic names a recognizable situation, a consequence the audience cares about, and a useful or surprising way through it. Specificity can qualify a buyer without explicitly saying their job title. “A supplier needs payment before your customer pays the invoice” creates a different audience than “grow your business.”

Separate the subject from the angle: the subject may be working capital; the angle may be why a busy company can still be unable to fund its next order. A format such as a skit supplies an expression, not a reason to care.

Ask what the viewer gains: recognition, a decision, a demonstrated method, a useful distinction, a reveal, or entertainment with a payoff. A true internal detail can still be a weak public idea. Research should discover audience language and alternative explanations, not just collect hooks that contain a keyword.

## Attention and promise

An opening sells the next moment. Build it from an audience/situation cue and a credible reason to continue. Curiosity works when the missing piece is specific enough to matter. Vague warnings, withheld nouns, and unexplained numbers create confusion rather than useful curiosity.

Design the first frame, first spoken thought, and text together:

- Text can establish the situation while a physical action creates the discrepancy.
- Speech can pose the problem while a visible result makes it credible.
- Text can label a comparison while the actor demonstrates the two experiences.
- A relevant prop can make an abstract constraint visible before speech begins.

Do not force all three channels to repeat one sentence. Check their combined meaning muted, heard without picture, and at phone size. Each individual channel need not carry the entire argument; the combined version must be immediately legible.

Spectacle earns its place when the eventual explanation makes the opening feel necessary. If swapping in an unrelated product leaves the same spectacle intact, examine whether the idea has any product relevance.

## Retention, value, and payoff

Treat hook, retain and reward as functions of fulfilled expectations, not a license to stall. They may overlap: a comparison image can attract attention and provide value at once. Sequential video beats often distribute them over time. For each beat, know:

1. What question or expectation is active?
2. What new fact, demonstration, consequence, or reversal arrives here?
3. What remains worth watching for?

Use nested curiosity only when the viewer can still follow the main argument. Give partial answers, then advance to a more valuable question. A list can promise a final item while each earlier item is independently useful. A demonstration can reveal the result early and retain attention through explaining how it happened.

For each unit, pay off a concrete expectation before extending the argument. In a longer video, connect units by a real dependency: once the viewer understands the problem, they can assess the solution; once they see the result, they can learn the method. Track rewards by new understanding, useful action, felt recognition or earned entertainment, not a forced number of seconds. Hormozi's [official content lesson](https://www.acquisition.com/training/leads5) distinguishes repeated reward from endless unresolved loops.

The final payoff resolves the main promise. A CTA is not a substitute for that resolution. “Comment to get the answer” should not replace the answer explicitly promised inside the video.

## Argument and story

Stories need a situation, a goal or expectation, a meaningful obstacle/change, and a consequence or resolution. Choose only context required to understand that change. Specific actions and decisions do more work than abstract labels such as “chaos” or “transformation.”

Use causality where it exists: an obstacle changes the next decision; that decision changes the outcome. Do not mechanically insert “but” and “so” into unrelated facts. For explanation, demonstrate a mechanism or distinction; for story, let the decision and consequence reveal the lesson.

A beat earns time through evidence, tension, explanation, characterization, contrast, or payoff. Cut recap lines that simply restate what the previous sentence already established. Preserve useful connective language when cutting it would make speech choppy or logic harder to follow.

## Paid persuasion

Nathan Perdriau's problem education and product-bridge teachings are especially useful for cold audiences: recognized problem → why it happens → what a useful solution must do → product fit → evidence/objections → appropriate next step. The exact order and length depend on awareness, format, and available proof.

Alex Hormozi's hook work emphasizes a relevant callout and the value available for attention. His ad teaching supports generating materially different hooks around a stable persuasive body and matching a believable offer to the buyer's concern. Variety of punctuation is not variety of angle.

Daniel Iles' attention-first approach applies when the campaign deliberately buys relevant attention for a later funnel step. Track the downstream audience quality and total acquisition cost. An ad tasked with producing qualified leads or purchases must give viewers sufficient reason and information to take that step. The funnel's job resolves the choice; a coach's broad slogan does not.

Do not turn either creator's teaching into an unconditional “hide the product” rule. Product-aware retargeting, comparison, demonstration, catalog, or offer ads may benefit from immediate product visibility and naming. Showing a product early also differs from interrupting the story with a premature sales pitch.

The [inspected paid examples](paid-cases.md) include an immediate Viral Coach offer, Hormozi education excerpts without a spoken CTA, and Ava comparison videos used as ads. Choose the ad's job from the actual brief/account, not from the creator's general slogan or ad-library presence alone.

Mechanism and proof are not interchangeable. Explaining why an offer could help establishes plausibility; evidence supports whether the relevant claim is true. When proof is missing, use accurate explanation and a modest claim, or request the missing evidence. Do not fabricate a testimonial or stage a synthetic outcome as actual experience.

## Delivery and editing

Delivery changes the meaning of the words. Plan stance, intention, eye line, emphasis, breathing room, reaction, and movement where they matter. A physical action should have an achievable start state, action, and end state. A camera angle should reveal something or establish a relationship, not merely be unusual.

Editing supports comprehension, feeling, and continuity. Additional cuts are useful only when they improve that job. A held shot can make a reveal or statement stronger. Treat music, SFX, captions, framing, and B-roll as separate decisions. Avoid repeating identical verbal and visual explanations unless repetition helps learning.

Observe negative examples and deliberate restraint in references. A creator's recurring zoom or caption treatment may be a house style, not the feature that separates their strongest work.

## Transfer and uncertainty

Model the decision a reference makes: when a visual comparison replaces an explanation, when a reveal pays off an opening, or when a demonstration answers an objection. Transfer the structure using the new brand's facts, speaker, and assets. Do not transplant someone else's numbers, biography, audience trust, or proprietary footage.

An account's best and worst sampled posts reveal associations. They do not isolate creative quality from distribution, timing, topic demand, audience history, paid promotion, or platform changes. Use observed patterns to nominate tests; keep causal wording for evidence that supports it.
