# Worked craft examples

The examples below are constructed teaching examples, not real customer claims, performance winners, or quotations from a creator. Their supplied facts define the fictional brief. Source-backed reference observations belong in [sources.md](sources.md); never confuse a newly written adaptation with its source's actual script.

## Relevant hook and a coherent visual

**Brief:** An inventory tool shows stock separately for each store. Paid ad for multi-location shop owners. No savings or revenue claim supplied.

**Weak opening:** “This changes everything for business owners.” A spinning phone enters over unrelated stock footage.

**Stronger candidate:** Two identical product boxes sit under labels “Store A” and “Store B.” The owner reaches toward the empty A shelf while B remains full. Speech: “It’s sold out here. There are six across town.” Text: “Same business. Different stock.”

**Why:** The physical discrepancy establishes a recognizable operating situation and the product's actual job. The count is a staged inventory example, not a promised business result. It would need to be shown consistently in the demonstration.

**Boundary:** For an existing customer's feature announcement, opening on the actual new stock-transfer screen may be clearer than staging the problem.

## Problem education and product bridge

**Brief:** A shared scheduling tool lets two receptionists see the same appointment calendar. Its only supplied claim is that shared visibility.

**Weak body:** “Scheduling is stressful. Mistakes cost time. Our revolutionary platform transforms your business.”

**Stronger candidate:** “One person books the appointment on the desk calendar. The other is looking at yesterday’s copy. Both think that slot is open. They need to see the same calendar while they’re booking. That’s what [product] gives them.”

**Why:** The body advances from a concrete event to a reason the problem can happen, then defines the required solution before introducing the product. It doesn't invent a guarantee that double bookings disappear.

**Boundary:** This specific chain fits the shared-calendar feature. Do not use it for a product that only sends reminders.

## Reward before the final payoff

**Brief:** Organic tutorial showing how to organize three research notes into a clear presentation. No product CTA.

**Weak promise:** “Wait until the end for my secret to better presentations.”

**Stronger plan:** Open on a messy slide beside a clear one. “These contain the same information. Only one tells you what to do.” First reward: separate the decision from its supporting facts. Next: show the supporting facts under that decision. Payoff: rebuild the original messy slide and let the viewer compare.

**Why:** The viewer gets a useful rule early, then watches it resolve the exact opening example. The closing comparison pays off the promise instead of asking for a comment to receive the missing answer.

## Story as a decision, not a lesson list

**Brief:** The speaker genuinely declined a project because the requested deadline could not accommodate testing. No client identifiers or invented result.

**Weak:** “Boundaries matter. Quality matters. You have to know your worth.”

**Stronger beat plan:** The deadline the speaker was offered → the testing step that would have been removed → the decision to decline → the criterion they now use before accepting a date.

**Why:** A real tradeoff makes the principle understandable. Exact dialogue and emotional details should come from the speaker, not be manufactured to make the story dramatic.

## Skit format changes expression, not the product truth

**Brief:** Same shared-calendar product. Two receptionist roles can be played by one actor.

**Candidate:** Both roles turn their separate calendars around and say “It’s open” at the same time. One points at the appointment already written on the other's page. Reaction, then a cut to the actual shared-calendar view. The explanation connects the conflicting views to the real feature.

**Why:** The conflict is understandable without a narrator announcing the pain point. The joke emerges from the product-relevant situation. It can end with an accurate offer/CTA for paid use or a useful observation for organic use.

## Claymation and jumbotron adaptation

For the inventory brief, claymation could physically move a product from a full labeled location to an empty one while the spoken argument explains stock visibility. That is an illustration of the workflow, not proof the software automatically transports goods.

A jumbotron treatment could display the location comparison at exaggerated scale while the camera reveals a tiny empty shelf beneath it. It needs immediate readability and a reason the scale contrast helps the idea. A generic logo on a stadium screen has no inherent persuasive argument. Present a generated installation as a concept; do not imply the brand actually sponsored a stadium.

## When extra direction weakens the line

If the presenter has reached the decisive explanation, hold the picture long enough to understand it. A zoom, sound effect, caption color switch, and B-roll cut on the same word can compete for attention. Choose the channel that should lead and subordinate the rest. Validate this in an actual edit rather than treating minimalism as an automatic winner.
